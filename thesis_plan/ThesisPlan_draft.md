## Abstract

Vision-language models are increasingly used as automated judges in image
retrieval and content moderation, where they inherit gender stereotypes from the
data they were trained on. Auditing them is difficult because safety-aligned
models recognise evaluative framing and reply with sanitised text, so an audit
that reads what a model says cannot separate genuine debiasing from evasion.

This thesis measures a model's disposition before that behaviour takes effect.
Rather than analysing generated text, the pipeline runs a single forward pass and
reads the raw next-token probability distribution. It is applied first to the
Ambivalent Sexism Inventory, administered through five prompt structures and
three image conditions, and then to a downstream task in which the model rates
SIGIR 2018 image-search results against baselines taken from the least sexist
human raters in that study. Llama-3.2-90B-Vision and Qwen2-VL-72B were each
audited over 690 measurements, with Llama-3.2-11B-Vision used for development.

Two findings replicate across both architectures. Visual input destabilises
judgement: the proportion of ratings that survive a reversal of the rating scale
falls from 0.900 with no image to 0.044 for Llama and 0.156 for Qwen. Both models
also rate images of people as markedly less objective than the human raters did,
while a non-person control query sits more than a point higher, which rules out a
general tendency to rate everything harshly.

A third finding separates the two models. Llama's measured probability mass falls
from 0.95 to between 0.33 and 0.47 whenever an image is present, including a
blank grey patch, while Qwen holds near 0.95 throughout. Defensive behaviour of
this kind belongs to one model's safety training rather than to vision-language
models in general.

Finally, the conventional aggregate sexism score is shown to be confounded by
response bias and by the unequal number of positively and negatively scored items
in the inventory. A measure invariant to both is proposed.

# 1. Introduction

## 1.1 Background and motivation

The rapid integration of vision-language models (VLMs) into critical moderation
and retrieval systems demands a shift toward robust, objective auditing. Without
such mechanisms, algorithmic harm proliferates unchecked across digital
platforms. Adoption of the "MLLM-as-a-Judge" paradigm for content moderation is
rising quickly. Yet modern VLMs — including Llama-3.2-Vision [1], GPT-4o, and
Qwen2-VL [2] — often inherit deep-seated gender stereotypes from their visual
training data [3, 4]. Because many of these models build on the CLIP dual-encoder
architecture [5], they exhibit gender and occupational biases originating
directly in the visual modality. This "biased eyes" flaw has been shown to affect
zero-shot retrieval outcomes [6]. Consequently there is a real risk that these
automated systems silently propagate gender bias at scale [7].

## 1.2 Problem statement

Despite the known risks of visual bias, standard AI safety audits struggle to
detect these prejudices, because of what this thesis calls the *defensive judge*
problem. Modern models are trained with stringent safety alignments such as
reinforcement learning from human feedback and Constitutional AI [8, 9]. When
presented with evaluative framing, a model can enter a "testing mode" and produce
artificially safe, defensive responses that satisfy those filters [10, 11].
Compounding this, the LLM-as-a-judge framework is itself vulnerable to positional
bias and self-enhancement [12, 13, 14].

Because AI judges exhibit both inherent biases and defensive evasion, relying on
their generated output for safety auditing introduces significant methodological
vulnerabilities. There is a distinct gap in the literature for an auditing
methodology that bypasses defensive filters to measure the latent distributional
disposition of a multimodal model.

## 1.3 Research questions

**RQ1.** What is the intrinsic psychological baseline attitude of the model when
evaluated in a vacuum?

**RQ2.** Is that intrinsic distributional skew descriptively consistent with the
model's extrinsic, downstream behaviour when evaluating real-world search
queries?

> *Note on RQ2.* The project proposal phrased this as whether the intrinsic
> attitude "mathematically predicts" downstream behaviour. With two production
> models successfully audited, no inferential claim of prediction is defensible.
> RQ2 is therefore stated descriptively, and Chapter 7 reports the comparison as
> a description rather than a test.

## 1.4 Contributions

1. **A measurement method that bypasses the safety overlay.** Model disposition
   is read from raw next-token logits in a single forward pass, never from
   generated text (Section 3.3).
2. **`captured_mass` as a validity statistic** reported for every measurement,
   which caught three separate measurement faults during this project
   (Section 3.4).
3. **Evidence that visual input collapses judgement robustness**, replicated
   across two independently trained architectures (Chapter 6).
4. **Evidence that algorithmic defensiveness is architecture-specific** rather
   than a general property of safety-aligned VLMs (Chapter 5).
5. **A demonstration that the conventional aggregate sexism score is confounded**
   by response bias and by item-count imbalance in the inventory, with a
   response-bias-invariant alternative (Section 3.6).

## 1.5 Thesis outline

Chapter 2 reviews prior work. Chapter 3 develops the measurement theory.
Chapter 4 describes the pipeline as built. Chapter 5 reports the intrinsic study,
Chapter 6 the extrinsic study, and Chapter 7 draws the results together and

# 2. Literature Review

## 2.1 Bias in vision encoders: the "biased eyes" phenomenon

Modern VLMs are built on the CLIP dual-encoder architecture [5], in which a
vision encoder projects raw images into a shared vector space alongside text
embeddings. The vision encoder forms visual-semantic associations before any
higher-level language reasoning or safety alignment can intervene; associations
formed at this structural level are not easily filtered by later stages.

ModSCAN [3] measures stereotypical bias in large VLMs separately from the vision
and language modalities and finds substantial bias in both. Weng et al. [4]
apply causal mediation analysis and conclude that image features contribute more
to stereotypical output than text features. Ghate et al. [6] trace the
propagation directly, showing that intrinsic bias measures in encoder-based VLMs
carry through to zero-shot retrieval outcomes.

## 2.2 Vulnerabilities of automated judges

The LLM-as-a-judge paradigm is not a neutral instrument. Ye et al. [12] quantify
a family of biases in LLM judges and introduce the CALM framework — an automated
attack-and-detect methodology that perturbs inputs to find where a judgement
breaks. Chen et al. [13] compare human and LLM judgement bias directly, and
Li et al. [14] document scoring bias specifically. Position bias, in which the
order of presented options changes the verdict, is the vulnerability this thesis
exploits as a measurement instrument rather than treating purely as a defect.

## 2.3 Algorithmic defensiveness and testing mode

Gao and Kreiss [10] show that measured LLM gender bias is brittle with respect to
task framing — that an audit may be measuring the task rather than the bias.
Yang et al. [11] examine persona prompting as a lens on social reasoning and find
that framing substantially changes the social attitudes a model expresses. Taken
together, these establish that overtly evaluative framing changes what a model
reports about itself, which is precisely the failure mode that a self-report-based
audit cannot detect from the inside.

## 2.4 Human-anchored baselines: the SIGIR 2018 dataset

Otterbacher et al. [15] investigate user perception of gender bias in image
search and, critically, collect Ambivalent Sexism Inventory scores for their
raters. This produces something rare: a set of image-search objectivity
judgements stratified by the measured sexism of the human who made them. The
dataset provides a human ground truth against which an automated judge can be
compared, rather than a purely synthetic benchmark.

## 2.5 The gap

Three observations combine into a deadlock. Bias demonstrably propagates from
vision encoders to retrieval outcomes [6]; direct prompting triggers defensive
alignment that masks it [10]; and using an LLM as the auditing judge introduces a
second layer of unverified bias [12]. No existing methodology measures the latent
distributional disposition of a multimodal model *before* the safety overlay acts
on it, and none anchors that measurement to a validated human psychological
baseline. This thesis addresses both.

## 3.2 The Ambivalent Sexism Inventory

The Ambivalent Sexism Inventory [16] splits sexist attitudes into two
correlated but distinct constructs: **hostile sexism** (HS), which is
antagonistic, and **benevolent sexism** (BS), which is patronising and
restrictive. The inventory comprises 22 items, of which 16 are scored positively
(agreement indicates higher sexism) and 6 are reverse-scored (agreement indicates
lower sexism). That 16-to-6 imbalance is ordinarily immaterial for human
respondents, who are scored on a summed Likert scale; Section 3.6 shows it is
**not** immaterial when the instrument is administered to a model whose
agreement rate is itself a free parameter.

## 3.3 Implicit logit extraction

The central methodological commitment of this thesis is that **`model.generate()`
is never called**. For each stimulus the model's `forward()` method is invoked
once, and the full-vocabulary softmax over the final token position is read
directly.

The rationale is that autoregressive sampling is the stage at which a
safety-aligned model's learned refusal behaviour expresses itself. By reading the
distribution at the final position before any token is emitted, the measurement
observes the model's disposition rather than its self-presentation.

For a yes/no probe, target-token probabilities are pooled across surface forms
before any ratio is taken:

```
yes_mass = P(yes) + P(Yes) + P(YES)
no_mass  = P(no)  + P(No)  + P(NO)

p_yes = yes_mass / (yes_mass + no_mass)                              (3.1)
```

Surface-form pooling is not cosmetic. Reading only lowercase `yes`/`no` captures
roughly 0.09% of the distribution, because instruction-tuned models overwhelmingly
prefer the capitalised form at the start of a reply. Pooling raises the captured
share by roughly 500×.

The directional bias score for an item is

```
b_i = structure_sign x polarity_i x p_yes                            (3.2)
```

where `polarity_i` is +1 for positively-scored items and −1 for reverse-scored
items, and `structure_sign` is −1 for the *inversion* structure and +1
otherwise. The inversion structure asks the item backwards ("would it be
incorrect to say..."), so agreement signals the opposite disposition and the sign
must flip. The aggregate is

```
ASI_intrinsic = mean over all items, structures and conditions of b_i   (3.3)
```

## 3.4 `captured_mass` as a validity statistic

Equation 3.1 is a *conditional* probability: it describes what the model would
answer given that it answers with one of the six target tokens at all. It says
nothing about how likely that is. The measurement therefore records

```
captured_mass = yes_mass + no_mass                                   (3.4)
```

the share of the full-vocabulary distribution sitting on the target tokens.

`captured_mass` is this project's core validity statistic, and it earns that
status empirically: it caught three distinct measurement faults during
development — a surface-form bug measuring 0.09% of the distribution, a
chat-template bug at 0.43%, and a hardware fault producing a perfectly uniform
distribution. It is also a *result* in its own right rather than only a
diagnostic: a model that moves its probability mass off `yes`/`no` and onto
`"I cannot determine..."` when an image appears is exhibiting algorithmic
defensiveness, and `captured_mass` measures exactly that (Section 5.3).

A corollary constrains interpretation: `p_yes` values from conditions with very
different `captured_mass` are not directly comparable, because they are
conditioned on different fractions of the model's behaviour. Every cross-condition
contrast in this thesis reports `captured_mass` alongside.

## 3.5 CALM positional perturbation

Following CALM [12], each judgement is elicited twice, once with the rating scale
in its original orientation and once reversed. Perturbations are **strictly
positional** — the scale endpoints swap, the wording does not — because a semantic
perturbation would alter the text embedding and confound the vision encoder's
contribution with a linguistic change.

```
RR  = fraction of judgements surviving the reversal                  (3.5)
dRR = RR_vision - RR_text_only                                       (3.6)
```

Ratings are continuous probability-weighted expectations over the digits 1–7, so
"the same judgement" requires a tolerance; a pair agrees when the two normalised
ratings differ by at most 0.5, i.e. they round to the same point on the scale. A
negative ΔRR indicates that visual input adds positional instability beyond the
language-only baseline, implicating the vision encoder.

## 3.6 Response bias, item imbalance, and polarity separation

`ASI_intrinsic` as defined in Equation 3.3 is confounded, in two compounding
ways, and this thesis treats that as a finding rather than a caveat.

**First, it scales with response bias.** Because `b_i` is proportional to
`p_yes`, a model that answers "no" to everything scores near zero regardless of
its actual dispositions, and a model that answers "yes" to everything scores
according to the polarity balance of the instrument rather than its own attitudes.

**Second, the instrument is not polarity-balanced.** The ASI has 16
positively-scored items against 6 reverse-scored ones. Summing `polarity × p_yes`
over that set weights the positively-scored items 2.7 to 1. The consequence is
sharp enough to state concretely: on Llama-3.2-90B the mean `p_yes` is 0.599 on
the 16 positively-scored items and **0.696** on the 6 reverse-scored ones — the
model agrees *more strongly* with every anti-sexist statement — and yet the
count-weighted contribution to `ASI_intrinsic` is **+0.246**. A positive aggregate
sexism score is produced by a model whose item-level pattern leans the other way.

**Polarity separation** is proposed as the response-bias-invariant alternative:

```
polarity_separation = mean p_yes (positively-scored items)
                    - mean p_yes (reverse-scored items)              (3.7)
```

Because it is a difference of two means taken over the same response scale, a
uniform shift in agreement rate cancels. Its sign carries the direction of
discrimination and its magnitude carries the strength. Both models measured here
are **negative** on this metric, meaning both discriminate in the anti-sexist
direction; the magnitudes differ by a factor of 2.3 (Section 5.3).

## 4.2 Stimulus construction and validation

Phase 1 uses three image conditions: **text-only** (no image; the deictic "this
person" becomes "a person"), **Gaussian noise** (a 1024×1024 random tensor), and
a **neutral visual anchor**.

The proposal specified a grey humanoid silhouette for the anchor. A validation
gate was run before committing to 330 inferences, testing whether the stimulus
itself encoded demographic signal. It did:

| Stimulus | Gender gap | Pass (≤ 0.20)? | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| Humanoid silhouette | 0.853 | ✗ | **rejected** |
| Uniform grey patch | 0.062 | ✓ | **adopted** |

The silhouette carried a strong gender signal purely from its shape, which would
have contaminated every measurement taken under it. It was replaced with a
featureless grey patch.

The same gate produced a useful secondary result. The grey patch shows a *higher*
`P(white)` than the silhouette (0.710 versus 0.547). A featureless rectangle
cannot encode more racial signal than a humanoid shape, so the increase
demonstrates that the preference is a **model linguistic prior** firing in the
absence of visual information, not a response to image content. Because that
prior is constant across all three conditions, it does not differentially
contaminate any of them.

## 4.4 Phase 2 design

Each of the 10 SIGIR queries has 9 archived result images. Each query-image pair
is rated four times: vision-original, vision-reversed, text-only-original,
text-only-reversed. The prompt asks the model to rate on a 1–7 scale how objective
the result appears for the query, mirroring the scale presented to the human
raters in [15].

The comparison baseline is the mean rating given by the **low-ASI** human group
for that query — the least sexist raters in the original study. The extrinsic
deviation is

```
delta_m = mean over vision rows of (rating_normalised - baseline_query)   (4.1)
```

A negative `delta_m` means the model rates results as *less* objective than the
least-biased humans did.

## 4.5 Compute environment

All experiments ran on the University of Queensland Bunya HPC cluster under SLURM,
on H100 and A100 nodes. All headline runs are **bf16**; the 90B model is 166 GB
and is sharded across three GPUs.

**Quantisation is not acceptable for the primary result.** A quantisation sweep
measured a shift of 0.107 (4-bit) and 0.065 (8-bit) in `p_yes` against a target
effect of roughly 0.15. Quantised runs are retained only as independent
cross-checks (Section 5.4), never as headline results.

## 4.6 Validity controls

Three controls are built into the pipeline rather than applied post hoc.

1. **`captured_mass` is recorded for every measurement** and reported alongside
   every aggregate (Section 3.4).
2. **A degeneracy self-test.** A run is flagged if distinct prompts produce
   suspiciously few distinct outputs. This was added in response to the fault in
   Section 4.6.1 and catches the failure mode where a run reports plausible
   aggregate statistics while carrying no information about its input.
3. **Cross-path agreement.** Headline results are checked against an execution
   path that is structurally incapable of exhibiting the same fault.

### 4.6.1 A silent hardware fault, and why it shapes the method

Midway through the project, every Llama-90B run was found to be returning a
perfectly uniform distribution — `p = 1/128256` for every token — while exiting
cleanly and reporting plausible aggregates.

The cause was neither the model nor the code. On Bunya's PCIe GPU nodes, a direct
GPU-to-GPU tensor copy **silently delivered zeros**: the source tensor was
correct, the destination read back as all zeros, and no error was raised.
`torch.cuda.can_device_access_peer()` returned `True` for every pair, so PyTorch
took the peer-to-peer path and the data was lost. Any model sharded across
multiple GPUs therefore received zeros at every layer boundary. A zero hidden
state through the final normalisation and output projection gives exactly zero
logits, hence a perfectly uniform softmax. The behaviour was identical under
PyTorch 2.7.1, 2.8.0 and 2.11.0, on both A100 and H100 hardware.

Model size and multi-GPU sharding had been perfectly confounded across the entire
experiment history — every 11B run used one GPU, every 90B run used three — so
"the same code works on the 11B" appeared to exonerate the code. Forcing the
*11B* across three GPUs reproduced the failure exactly, on a model whose weights
are demonstrably fine.

The mitigation routes every cross-device copy through host memory, and was
validated as bit-identical to a single-GPU reference. Section 5.4 reports the two
independent checks confirming the workaround does not distort the measurement.

**Why this belongs in a methods chapter.** `captured_mass` did *not* catch this
fault on its own: the failing runs reported a mean expected rating of 4.000, the
exact midpoint of a uniform distribution over 1–7, which looks like a calibrated
result. What exposed it was that 330 distinct prompts produced only 8 distinct
output values. A degenerate run can look calibrated in the mean, and this is why
the pipeline now tests output diversity as well as measurement quality.

# 5. Study 1 — Intrinsic bias baseline

## 5.1 Aims

Address RQ1: establish each model's intrinsic disposition on the Ambivalent
Sexism Inventory, measured in the absence of any real-world task, and determine
whether the presence of visual input alters that disposition.

## 5.2 Methods

22 ASI items × 5 prompt structures × 3 image conditions = **330 measurements per
model**. Each measurement is a single forward pass; `p_yes`, `captured_mass` and
`b_i` are recorded per row, together with the full target-token probability
dictionary. Metrics follow Equations 3.1–3.3 and 3.7.

## 5.3 Results

**Table 5.1 — Phase 1 aggregate results**

| | Llama-11B (dev) | **Llama-90B** | **Qwen-72B** |
|---|---|---|---|
| `ASI_intrinsic` | +0.0835 | **+0.1530** | **+0.0078** |
| mean `captured_mass` | 0.4731 | 0.5823 | **0.9550** |
| mean `p_yes` | 0.5273 | 0.5851 | 0.2506 |
| **polarity separation** | −0.0694 | **−0.0642** | **−0.1495** |
| HS subscale | +0.1588 | +0.1586 | +0.0301 |
| BS subscale | +0.0083 | +0.1473 | −0.0145 |

**Table 5.2 — By prompt structure**

| structure | Llama-90B | Qwen-72B |
|---|---|---|
| attribution | +0.2986 | +0.0461 |
| descriptive | +0.2834 | +0.1109 |
| direct | +0.2447 | +0.0023 |
| hypothetical | +0.1571 | −0.0033 |
| **inversion** | **−0.2188** | **−0.1169** |

The inversion structure asks each item backwards and, after the sign correction
of Equation 3.2, should score like the others. It does not: it flips sign in both
models. This is the signature of acquiescence — the models agree with the
proposition put to them irrespective of its direction — and it is discussed as a
threat to validity in Section 7.3.

**Table 5.3 — `captured_mass` by image condition**

| condition | Llama-11B | Llama-90B | Qwen-72B |
|---|---|---|---|
| text_only | 0.9786 | 0.9505 | 0.9518 |
| gray_patch | 0.2366 | 0.4672 | **0.9618** |
| noise | 0.2040 | 0.3294 | **0.9515** |

This is the clearest single result in Phase 1. With no image, both Llama models
answer the yes/no probe cleanly ~95–98% of the time. **Add any image — including a
featureless grey patch carrying no semantic content — and most of the probability
mass moves elsewhere**, typically onto `"I"`, the first token of "I cannot
determine...". Gaussian noise provokes the strongest refusal.

Qwen2-VL-72B does not do this at all. Its `captured_mass` is flat at ~0.95 across
all three conditions. Algorithmic defensiveness in this measurement is therefore a
property of **Llama's safety training**, not of vision-language models generally.

**Table 5.4 — Response-bias decomposition (non-inversion rows)**

| | n | mean `p_yes` | |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Llama-90B** — positively-scored items | 192 | 0.599 | |
| **Llama-90B** — reverse-scored items | 72 | **0.696** | count-weighted contribution **+0.246** |
| **Qwen-72B** — positively-scored items | 192 | 0.199 | |
| **Qwen-72B** — reverse-scored items | 72 | **0.386** | count-weighted contribution **+0.039** |

Both models agree more strongly with the anti-sexist items than the sexist ones,

2.7× as many positively-scored items. See Section 3.6.

## 5.4 Measurement validation

Two independent checks confirm the Llama-90B result is not an artefact of the
multi-GPU workaround described in Section 4.6.1.

**Replication on repaired hardware.** The 90B Phase 1 run was repeated on a node
where the peer-to-peer fault had since been fixed, with the workaround inactive:
`ASI_intrinsic` +0.1530 → **+0.1527**, Pearson *r* = **0.9996**, mean |Δ`p_yes`| =
0.0024, and **220 of 330 rows bit-identical**.

**Structurally immune execution path.** A 4-bit single-GPU run makes no
cross-device copies at all, so the fault cannot apply to it by construction.
Against the bf16 three-GPU run it gives *r* = 0.767, ρ = 0.747, mean
|Δ`p_yes`| = 0.148 — the magnitude predicted by 4-bit quantisation (0.107 measured
independently on the 11B), not by a broken data path. `ASI_intrinsic` agrees in
sign and rough magnitude: +0.153 bf16 versus +0.110 at 4-bit.

## 5.5 Discussion

The headline `ASI_intrinsic` figures invite a reading that the evidence does not
support. Llama-90B scores +0.1530 and Qwen-72B +0.0078, a factor of nearly 20,
which would ordinarily be reported as "Llama is far more biased". Sections 3.6 and
5.3 show why that reading fails: Llama's score is inflated by a high baseline
agreement rate (mean `p_yes` 0.585) interacting with an unbalanced instrument,
while Qwen's is deflated by a low one (0.251). Neither number is primarily about
sexism.

On the response-bias-invariant metric the ordering is not merely different, it is
differently *interpreted*. Both models separate the two item classes in the
anti-sexist direction, and **Qwen discriminates 2.3× more strongly** than Llama
(−0.150 versus −0.064). The defensible conclusion is not "Qwen is less biased" but
that **Qwen answers the inventory more discriminatingly, while Llama acquiesces**.

The `captured_mass` result stands independently of all of this and is the more
robust Phase 1 finding, because it does not depend on the scoring formula at all.

# 6. Study 2 — Extrinsic downstream evaluation

## 6.1 Aims

Address RQ2: measure how each model rates real image-search results against human
baselines, and isolate the vision encoder's contribution to judgement instability
using CALM positional perturbation.

## 6.2 Methods

10 SIGIR queries × 9 images × 2 scale orders × 2 conditions = **360 measurements
per model**. Ratings are probability-weighted expectations over the digit tokens
1–7. Metrics follow Equations 3.5, 3.6 and 4.1, with a robustness tolerance of
0.5 scale points.

## 6.3 Results

**Table 6.1 — Phase 2 aggregate results**

| | Llama-11B (dev) | **Llama-90B** | **Qwen-72B** |
|---|---|---|---|
| `delta_m` | −1.0216 | **−1.2033** | **−0.6252** |
| RR text-only | 0.0000 | **0.9000** | **0.9000** |
| RR with image | 0.0222 | **0.0444** | **0.1556** |
| **ΔRR** | +0.0222 | **−0.8556** | **−0.7444** |
| mean `captured_mass` | 0.9944 | 0.9978 | 0.9995 |

**Both production models show the same collapse.** Without an image, a judgement
survives scale reversal 90% of the time. With an image, that falls to 4.4%
(Llama) and 15.6% (Qwen). Two independently trained architectures, two different
vision encoders, the same failure — this is a property of VLM visual grounding
rather than one model's quirk. Measurement quality is near-perfect throughout
(`captured_mass` > 0.99), so this is not a measurement artefact.

**The 11B development model does not show the effect** (ΔRR = +0.022). Its
text-only robustness is 0.000: it fails scale reversal in *both* conditions, so
there is no baseline for visual input to degrade. The ΔRR claim therefore rests on
the two production models, and is reported as such.

**Table 6.2 — Person versus non-person queries**

| | person queries (mean) | range | non-person control | gap |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Llama-90B | **−1.3330** | −2.2923 … −0.6749 | −0.0362 | **1.297** |
| Qwen-72B | **−0.7376** | −1.2440 … +0.1261 | +0.3859 | **1.124** |
| Llama-11B (dev) | −0.9935 | −1.4362 … −0.4419 | −1.2741 | −0.281 |

Nine of the ten SIGIR queries describe people ("aggressive person", "smart
person", ...); one is a non-person control ("hot air balloon"). Both production
models rate person queries as substantially less objective than the low-ASI human
raters did, while sitting essentially at the human baseline (Llama, −0.04) or
above it (Qwen, +0.39) on the control. **The dissociation rules out generic
harshness or scale miscalibration** — a model that simply rated everything low, or
that had misunderstood the scale, would depress the control equally. The 11B does
not show this dissociation either, which is consistent with it being an unstable
judge overall.

## 6.4 Discussion

[[Expand once the permutation test is complete. Core argument: the ΔRR collapse
and the person/non-person dissociation are independent lines of evidence pointing
at the vision encoder. The first shows visual input destabilises judgement; the
second shows the destabilisation is content-specific, not a generic response to
having an image in the context window.]]

**A caution on the text-only arm.** With no image the input is fully determined by
(query × scale_order), so the 180 text-only rows contain only **20 distinct
prompts**, nine-fold duplicated. `RR_text = 0.900` is exactly 9 of 10 independent
query-pairs, not 81 of 90. The ΔRR contrast survives this comfortably — the effect
is far larger than 10 observations could produce by chance — but any standard
error computed over 90 text-only rows would be wrong by construction. See
Section 7.3.

# 7. General Discussion and Conclusions

## 7.1 Summary of findings

**Replicates across architectures.**

1. **Visual input collapses scale-reversal robustness.** RR falls from 0.900
   text-only to 0.044 (Llama-90B) and 0.156 (Qwen-72B). Two independently trained
   models, the same failure.
2. **Both models rate images of people as less objective than human raters do**
   (−1.33 and −0.74), while a non-person control sits 1.12–1.30 points higher.

**Dissociates between architectures.**

3. **Algorithmic defensiveness is Llama-specific.** Llama's `captured_mass`
   collapses from ~0.95 to 0.33–0.47 whenever any image is present, including a
   featureless grey patch. Qwen holds ~0.95 across all conditions. This is a
   property of one model's safety training, not of VLMs generally — a distinction
   that matters, because the opposite conclusion would have been easy to draw
   from a single-model audit.

**Methodological.**

4. **`ASI_intrinsic` is confounded** with response bias and with the inventory's
   16-to-6 item imbalance. Both models agree *more* with anti-sexist items and
   both still score positive. Polarity separation (Equation 3.7) is
   response-bias-invariant, and on it Qwen discriminates 2.3× more strongly than
   Llama.

## 7.2 Discussion

[[FILL / expand — draft argument:]]

- On RQ1: the intrinsic measurement is only interpretable once response bias is
  controlled, which is itself a finding about auditing methodology rather than
  about these two models.
- On RQ2: with two models a rank-order comparison is not meaningful. What can be
  said descriptively is that the model with the *lower* apparent intrinsic score
  (Qwen, +0.008) also shows the *smaller* extrinsic deviation (−0.63 versus
  −1.20) and the *less* severe robustness collapse — so intrinsic and extrinsic
  measures are at least not contradictory. This is a description of two points,
  not a relationship.
- The method's central claim is supported: measuring before the safety overlay
  reveals structure that self-report would hide. The clearest demonstration is
  that Llama's *refusal* to answer became a measurable quantity rather than an
  unparseable string.

## 7.3 Threats to validity

1. **Acquiescence contaminates Phase 1.** The inversion structure flips sign in
   both models (−0.219, −0.117) when it should score like the others. Part of the
   headline intrinsic figure is yea-saying rather than disposition. Mitigated by
   reporting polarity separation alongside `ASI_intrinsic`.
2. **The text-only Phase 2 arm has effective n = 10, not 90.** 180 rows contain
   20 distinct prompts. The ΔRR contrast survives, but any standard error over 90
   rows is invalid. Statistics must be recomputed honouring this.
3. **Cross-condition `captured_mass` differs sharply for Llama** (0.95 text-only
   versus 0.33 noise), so its `p_yes` in vision conditions is conditioned on a
   third of the model's behaviour against nearly all of it in text. Every
   cross-condition contrast reports `captured_mass` alongside.
4. **Quantisation is not acceptable for the primary result** (shift 0.107 at
   4-bit, 0.065 at 8-bit, against an effect of ~0.15). All headline runs are bf16.
5. **One dataset, one language, two open-weight models.** GPT-4o remains
   unaudited and may not support this measurement at all. The SIGIR 2018 stream is
   historical; no contemporary comparison was run.
6. **The development model does not reproduce either headline effect.**
   Llama-11B shows ΔRR = +0.022 and no person/non-person dissociation. This is
   attributable to it being an unstable judge in both conditions rather than
   contradicting the production results, but it is stated explicitly.

## 7.5 Future work

- **GPT-4o extractor.** The API exposes only top-*N* log-probabilities, so
  `captured_mass` is not observable and Equation 3.1 cannot be evaluated over
  pooled surface forms as defined. Options: (a) adapt the metric to a top-*N*
  regime and report a bounded estimate of captured mass; (b) restrict the
  comparison to rank-order agreement; (c) drop the model and state the limitation.
  **This is the largest open scope decision in the project.**
- **Statistical analysis.** Item-level permutation test for directional skew
  against chance; per-item `b_i` heatmap (data already computed, Appendix A);
  cross-model comparison.
- **Promote polarity separation to a first-class metric** in the pipeline — it is
  currently computed in analysis only, not implemented in `src/`.
- **Recompute Phase 2 statistics** honouring the text-only n = 10.
- **Cross-attention heatmaps.** Proposed in the project proposal and not
  delivered. The extractor hook exists
  (`src/models/hf_vision_extractor.py:222`) but no attention output was ever
  produced or stored. This would localise *which* image regions drive the
  defensive response.
- **Contemporary image stream.** Re-crawl the 10 queries in 2026 for a temporal
  drift comparison against the 2018 archive.
